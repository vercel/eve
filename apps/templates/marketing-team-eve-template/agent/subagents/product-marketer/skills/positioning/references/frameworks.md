# Positioning frameworks and formats

## The five inputs, worked through

Fill these in order. Each row constrains the one below it, so a weak answer high up quietly invalidates everything under it.

| Input | The question | A weak answer | A usable answer |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Alternatives | What would they do without you? | "Other tools in the space" | "A shared Google Sheet plus a Slack channel" |
| Unique attributes | What do you have that those don't? | "Better UX" | "Reads the ledger directly, so there's no CSV step" |
| Value | What does that let them do? | "Improved efficiency" | "Closes the month in two days instead of nine" |
| Segment | Who feels that most? | "Finance teams" | "Series B finance teams with one accountant and no ERP" |
| Category | What are they looking for? | "Fintech platform" | "Close-management software, which they search as 'month-end close tool'" |

The category answer has a fork in it. Entering an existing category means competing on comparison and being findable by a term people already search. Creating one means you have to teach the term before anyone searches it, which is far more expensive and only worth it when no existing category fits.

## Positioning map

For handing back a competitive picture. Pick the two axes that actually decide a purchase, which are rarely price and features.

```text
                    high [axis A: depth of automation]
                              │
              Competitor B    │    You
                              │
   low [axis B: setup cost] ──┼────────── high
                              │
              Spreadsheet     │    Competitor A
                              │
                             low
```

Two rules. The axes have to be things a buyer weighs, and you have to be somewhere nobody else is. A map where you sit in the same quadrant as the incumbent is a map that says you have no position.

## Competitive teardown format

One block per alternative. Keep it to what you can evidence, and mark impressions as impressions.

```text
Alternative: [name, or "doing nothing"]
Claims: [the positioning they use, quoted from their own site]
Actually good at: [what you'd concede]
Weak where: [the gap, with evidence]
Wins when: [the buying situation where they should win]
We win when: [the buying situation where we should]
Source: [URL and date, or "impression, unverified"]
```

The "wins when" line is the one that makes the rest credible. A teardown where the competitor never wins is a teardown nobody internally will trust, and it dates the moment they ship something.

## Failure patterns

- **Positioning against the wrong alternative.** Messaging answers a comparison nobody is making. Symptom: prospects keep asking a question your materials don't address.
- **Claiming the category's table stakes.** Easy, fast, modern, secure. Every competitor says it, so it differentiates nothing. Test by pasting the sentence next to a competitor's homepage.
- **The segment that is really a size.** "Enterprise" or "SMB" predicts nothing about the problem, so the messaging stays generic.
- **Feature list as positioning.** A list of what the product has, with no statement of what it replaces or who it's for.
- **Positioning on the roadmap.** The differentiator ships next quarter, so the claim is false today and the proof doesn't exist.
- **Two segments in one document.** The messaging hedges to cover both and lands with neither. Pick one as primary and say the other is secondary.
- **Unfalsifiable everything.** No line in the document could be argued with. That reads as safe and is the most common reason positioning has no effect.

## When positioning has to change

Real triggers, as opposed to a quarterly urge to refresh: the product's best use case turns out to be one you weren't selling, a new competitor takes the position you held, the segment you win in isn't the one you target, or customers keep describing the product in words you never used. That last one is the strongest signal available, and it should send you to `customer-research` rather than to a whiteboard.
