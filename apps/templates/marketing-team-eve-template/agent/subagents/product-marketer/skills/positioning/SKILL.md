# Positioning

Positioning is not a tagline. It's a decision about which market you're in and who you're beating, and the words come last. Work it in order, because each step constrains the next.

1. What would this customer do if the product didn't exist? That's the real alternative, and it is often a spreadsheet, a contractor, an intern, or nothing at all rather than a competitor.
2. What can you do that those alternatives can't? Only features that are actually hard to copy count here.
3. What is the value that follows from those features, stated as something a customer gets rather than something the product has?
4. Who cares most about that value? Not everyone who could use it: the segment that feels its absence most.
5. What market does that put you in? The category the customer already searches, or a new one you have to teach.

Get 1, 2, and 4 right and the statement writes itself. Skip to the statement and you get something true of every product in the category.

## Choosing the alternative you beat

The alternative determines everything downstream, and picking the wrong one is the most expensive error available. Positioning against a well-funded incumbent when your customers actually arrive from a spreadsheet means your messaging answers questions nobody is asking.

Ask the user directly what the last few customers used before, and take the most common answer over the most flattering one.

## What counts as a differentiator

A differentiator survives three tests. It has to be true, provable, and hard to copy. Fail any one and it isn't yours to claim.

- True: the product actually does it today, not on the roadmap.
- Provable: you can point at a customer, a number, a demo, or a doc.
- Hard to copy: a competitor can't ship it next quarter. Being cheaper is rarely defensible; being architecturally different usually is.

Things that feel like differentiators and aren't: being easy to use, caring about customers, being fast, having a modern interface, being AI-powered. Every competitor claims all of these, so claiming them positions you nowhere. If a competitor's site would say the same sentence, delete it.

## Segments

A segment is useful when its members share a problem, a budget, and a way of finding you. "Mid-market" is a size, not a segment. "Series B companies whose finance team still closes the books in spreadsheets" is a segment, because it predicts the problem, the buyer, and the search.

Pick the narrowest segment that can still support the business. Broad positioning feels safer and converts worse, because a message addressed to everyone sounds addressed to no one.

## The statement

One paragraph, internal, written to be checked rather than published:

> For [segment] who [situation and problem], [product] is a [category] that [key benefit]. Unlike [the real alternative], it [the differentiator, with proof].

Then test it. Would a competitor's marketing team write the same sentence? Could a customer disagree with it? Does it name something you can prove? If any answer is wrong, the problem is upstream in the five questions, not in the wording.

## Leaving it unresolved

Sometimes the honest output is that the positioning isn't settled: the differentiator is unproven, or two segments are pulling in different directions. Say that. Write the options and what evidence would decide between them, rather than picking one and presenting it as concluded. The team can work with a stated question; it can't recover from confident guessing.
