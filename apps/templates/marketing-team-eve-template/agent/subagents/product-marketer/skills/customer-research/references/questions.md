# Question sets

Grouped by what you're trying to learn. Ask three at a time, in the user's own terms, and follow the specifics rather than working through a list.

## Setting up brand context from scratch

The minimum to write a usable first draft. Everything else can come later.

1. In one sentence, what does the product do, and for whom?
2. Who was the most recent customer to buy, and what made them buy then?
3. What were they doing before? Be specific: a tool, a spreadsheet, a person, nothing.
4. What can you do that the alternatives can't, that they couldn't ship next quarter?
5. Who should not buy this?
6. How do you want to sound, and is there a company whose writing you'd point at?

Question 5 does the most work. An answer means the positioning is real. "Anyone could use it" means the segment isn't decided, and the document should say so rather than guess.

## Finding the real alternative

1. If the product disappeared tomorrow, what would your customers do on Monday?
2. What's in the "other" column when you ask new customers what they used before?
3. Which deals did you lose in the last quarter, and to what?
4. When a prospect goes quiet, what do you think they did instead?

The last question surfaces "nothing", which is the most common and most overlooked alternative. Positioning against inertia is a different job from positioning against a competitor.

## Testing a differentiator

1. Which competitors could build this in a quarter if they decided to?
2. What breaks for a customer who tries to do this without you?
3. What's the proof you'd show a skeptical buyer?
4. Has a customer ever said this specific thing back to you?

If the answer to 4 is no, the differentiator is internal and hasn't been validated as something customers notice.

## Segment

1. Which customers get the most out of this, and what do they have in common beyond size?
2. Which ones churned or never activated, and what did they have in common?
3. Who finds you on their own, versus who you had to go find?
4. Which customer would be hardest to replace, from their side?

The churn answer is usually more informative than the success answer, because it draws the boundary.

## Objections

1. What comes up in nearly every sales conversation?
2. What do you find yourself explaining twice?
3. What's the objection you don't have a good answer to?
4. Which customers were right not to buy?

Question 3 is uncomfortable and it's the reason to ask. An objection with no good answer either needs a product change or needs to be handled by narrowing the segment, and both are decisions worth surfacing.

## Voice

1. Show me two pieces of your own writing you were happy with.
2. What does your team sound like when it's being honest rather than promotional?
3. What phrases are banned, whether for legal reasons or taste?
4. Whose marketing in your space do you dislike, and why?

Question 4 gets a sharper answer than asking what they like, and the dislikes convert directly into the anti-list in `messaging`.

## Following up on a vague answer

Reusable prompts for pushing once without interrogating:

- "Faster than what, and by how much?"
- "Which customer, most recently?"
- "How would someone check that?"
- "What would have to be true for that to be wrong?"
- "What did they actually say?"

## When the user doesn't know

Record it as an open question with what would resolve it, rather than filling it in. A brand context that says "primary segment not yet decided; the last six customers split between two" is honest and actionable. One that picks a segment to look complete sends every downstream specialist in a direction nobody chose.
